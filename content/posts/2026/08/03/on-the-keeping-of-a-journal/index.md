A couple weeks ago, I started keeping a journal.

{{% callout emoji="✋" %}}
I need to be upfront in that while, yes, I too was enamored by folks and influencers who have these supposed hyper-optimized, productivity-exploding journaling practices, this isn't what I'm doing – nor would I endorse that as a useful practice for most folks.
{{% /callout %}}

Like some folks, I happened to stumble upon a [video by a prop-designing fella named Perry Daniels](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_QVYfPnNOg4) where he sets forth a (not necessarily novel) framework for journaling. He's no productivity influencer, by the looks of it, and I never watched a video from him before, which means he's arguably more qualified than channels that might lean toward obsession about productivity hacks and systems and yadda, yadda, yadda.

## The Holy Grail of Journaling

To save you the watch, he outlines what he calls "the grail method" (inspired by Indiana Jones' diary). He specifies only one "real" rule, but there are some guidelines that are integral to the practice:

> 1. No to-do lists.

This rule comes up near the end of the video, but the only constraint Daniels puts on the method is that the journal (or this particular one) is not for productivity. He has some explanation in the video on the didactic, but the goal for this journal is to be **creative**. Other means, like post-it notes or scratchpads, are the correct mediums for tasks that are ultimately ephemeral, temporary.

> 2. Collect everything.

As opposed to a spread of journals where some might be for thoughts, others for sketches (if that's your thing), quotes, or simply reference material, Daniels takes the approach of a more "once-and-for-all" sort of journal. Given that creativity is the foremost goal, organizational overhead poses a greater obstacle to being creative. In turn, the sort of "mess" this could make means that one might be less inclined to self-editing and fighting with their inner critic on what and how to write.

> 3. Review constantly.

Daniels' cites David Allen's *Getting Things Done* where Allen states "your mind is for having ideas, not holding them." Daniels detracts some from Allen's logical conclusion of organized journaling and instead simplifies the meaning: the point of the journal is to get things out, in one space, for easy review. In this method, reviewing is integral as it allows for the resurfacing of older ideas that might interplay with things currently floating around in your brain, or even across disciplines. Further, Daniels insists that this isn't for the purpose of studying or memorizing your past writings, but to simply keep older material fresh for establishing new creative connections.

## Personal Revisions

I think Daniels' method is an excellent foundation to work from, but I needed some adjustments, both to meet my own tastes but also to avoid hitting ADHD-related roadblocks. Thus, my "method" (which I will give no name to) augments and reduces Daniels' further to be more accommodating.[^1]

### 1. Not for Productivity
Firstly, I preserve the totality of the "not for productivity" constraint. There's an age old in-joke from the ADHD community on how the cure for ADHD, suggested by many, is [to simply use a planner](https://old.reddit.com/r/ADHD/comments/pelip6/how_i_cured_my_adhd_permanently/). That's it, ADHD solved, forever, we can all go and live productive, neurotypical lives! If this was truly an axiom, especially for ADHD folks, this rule needn't be stated.

…buuut it must be stated, not only because productivity is not the primary goal of the journal, but also because the appearance of productivity tends to become a higher ideal than whether you're achieving anything useful in journaling. It's easier to abandon a journaling practice when you define it by adherence to an arbitrary metric. By all means, establish a habit, form loose structures to induce writing, establish "proper contexts" where writing belongs (like after the kids have gone to bed and you don't have anything occupying you), these are useful practices that can prove to be a boon to actually writing – ***but*** they must not define whether you're "doing it right." Having an off-day or deciding to skip writing because you've opted to make a blog post doesn't mean you committed some sort of sin and therefore your only option is to give up entirely so that you never have to come face-to-face with that shame again.

Practices around productivity can be beneficial, and some should be put in place so that you feel more inclined to write, especially if you're starting from ground zero, but these practices should never pose an obstacle to doing that. If it helps you, great, but the moment it creates any sort of friction, throw that practice and ideal away. Consider reshaping how you define "productivity" in this context. Think less of quality (i.e. doing it "right") and more of quantity (i.e. doing it, at all). It is much more rewarding to simply write without regard for organizational minutiae, especially when you might be writing more introspectively, where a more "raw" output carries greater significance than whether it looks pretty.

### 2. Collect… Whatever Sounds Good

What you decide you want to preserve for later and what's best "kept" as a fleeting thought or muse, do what feels right to you. Personally, I have mainly dedicated my journal to self-reflection, thought-dumping if you will. Things like reference material, something that is not inherently temporary nor inherently worth preserving, other mediums could serve this purpose better. Daniels' method exhorts you to keep your journal with you wherever you go, so that you always have the opportunity to dump whatever's on your mind whenever you want. While I don't think this is a bad practice, I already have established, convenient mechanisms for keeping reference notes or holding onto quotes. I have no desire to shift that into another medium where I take the risk of creating friction in writing those things down. Additionally, I feel as though I've defined the tenor of my journal, and it feels as though mixing reference material in would devalue or dilute what I'm getting out of the practice. It places my three-page reflection on feeling closeness to God in the same level as something like "cardinals are the birds that sound like lasers". It would further detract from the value I'd get both in writing and looking back on older entries. That being said…

### 3. Review… When You Feel So Inclined

Besides the productivity rule, Daniels' drives this guideline home the most. He considers it the most important part of the grail method. While I have no disagreements with the concept within that framework, this doesn't particularly suit my needs.

I don't consider myself a creative person in the traditional sense, my creativity is best found in functionality and pragmatics than in abstract expression. So while I wouldn't throw out the idea of reviewing in its entirety, I don't see a purpose in constantly reviewing my various laments or retellings of how my day went. I'm not seeking to form derivative works of those entries, therefore the extent of my reviewing is for mere recollection – to look back on those entries and observe any sort of contrast in disposition and headspace. Long term, I might wish to process previous entries into a digital format for further analysis, to distill them for a post here, or to use them in providing context to someone like a therapist or spiritual guide, but otherwise simply writing is more than satisfactory for me.

### 4. Get Comfortable

This might be the only guideline of my own that you might be best throwing out, but I feel it was important to me actually following through with this.

Let your journaling materials be what feels right. Of course, maybe don't be extravagant. You don't *need* a Moleskine or a Leuchtturm1917 journal, unless you are 100% certain that there is some real, empirical benefit to be found in it, like the paper quality helps avoid less ink bleed or whatever. For example, I wanted something that I could sort of loosely "lock" and had some desire for an ability to use notebook refills, but without Etsy-tier pricing. I ended up stumbling upon [this Walmart generic journal](https://www.walmart.com/ip/Pen-Gear-Black-Perforated-Simulated-Leather-Journal/15159952163) that offered a simple button clasp but happened to also be refillable.[^2] I ended up screwing out the button clasp because it made writing on the back sides of pages a bit cumbersome and instead threading a thinner ribbon of elastic through the cover flap, knotting it, and tying the elastic around the cover when I wasn't writing. Ideally, I'd maybe go for something like a thin magnet, but this is a happy medium.

I also put a lot of value into finding a pen that feels right. I'm a heavy-handed writer and the last pen I really enjoyed was the [Paper Mate InkJoy 700RT](https://www.papermate.com/pens/ballpoint-pens/paper-mate-inkjoy-700rt-retractable-ballpoint-pens-medium-point-1.0mm/SAP_1945911.html) back in high school. The link suggests that PM seems to still make these, but I haven't seen them stocked anywhere since they "revamped" (or, alternatively, "enshittified") the InkJoy line in the late 2010s. I've trialed a bunch of different pens from the Dr. Grip line to the Parker Jotter XL to my most recent preference of a Pilot Precise V7 refill in a Pilot G2 Limited body, but so far I've mostly settled with the Uni Jetstream RT. My hand still cramps when using it, but it's been the best so far in terms of writing feel and keeping a coherent visual aesthetic with the notebook itself. I might try the Alpha Gel grip variant and see where it gets me.

My point being: writing is only as fun as it feels - so make sure you got materials that are conducive to you writing. Don't let bad pens or less preferable notebooks get in the way of actually writing, but consider being modest too.

Ultimately, my "method" is to throw out anything that poses an obstacle to writing and to solidify the things that *help* me write. Of course, what works for me is no guarantee for you, but I wanted to share my thoughts in the hopes that it might help others establish a practice that clicks for them. Keeping a journal has been quite a rewarding practice for me, but I'd never get this far if I measured things by doing it right vs. just doing it. A forest is the sum of individual trees, not the sum of each tree's attributes. It's more rewarding to see the forest being established than to get every tree looking perfect.

All that being said, just write. Resolve or toss out whatever is in the way of you doing it. The best journal and method is not the one that gets the best of you, but the one that gets *you* – with whatever mess that might bring. Just do it™.[^3]
